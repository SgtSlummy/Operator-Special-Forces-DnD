# Raphael's Council: The Lanterns of Greyharbor

World and campaign framework, first edition

**Reading boundary:** This file contains author and DM secrets. Give players `PLAYER_WORLD_PRIMER.md`, which contains only the opening knowledge. The framework establishes story canon and future campaign behavior; writing it does not implement those behaviors in the current application.

## 1. The promise of the campaign

**Optional expedition:** [Behind the Veil](BEHIND_THE_VEIL_GM.md) adds Nareth, an island region with an invasion deadline and freely ordered operations. Its GM guide defines the bridge from the Last Hearth, the distinction between the Veil and the Concord, and Raphael's counsel there. Activate it through play or choose it as a standalone campaign; the opening canon below remains the default. Share only its [player briefing](BEHIND_THE_VEIL_PLAYER_BRIEFING.md) with players.

Every evening, Greyharbor lights its lanterns for those who have not come home.

For three hundred years, that has been a gesture of hope. Now the lanterns are going dark, and with each extinguished flame, someone vanishes from the city's memory. Their coats still hang beside doors. Their meals still sit on tables. Their families explain away the empty chairs.

Only a few people notice.

Raphael speaks to those few. He calls them his chosen, and asks them to carry a light into places that have begun to forget themselves.

The heroes belong to the Lantern Compact, a small company entrusted with difficult missions across a fractured frontier. They recover missing people, confront unnatural threats, protect witnesses, negotiate between rivals, and venture into places larger forces cannot enter without making the crisis worse. Their home remains Greyharbor, where friends remember their promises and every return changes what the next departure means.

The campaign's central question is: **What makes a world worth saving when you cannot preserve everything exactly as it was?**

The tone combines uncanny exploration, fellowship, dangerous adventure, and political tension. There is room for monsters, rival champions, daring rescues, strange ruins, festivals, humor, and quiet meals after difficult journeys. Hope grows through specific people and places the party learns to care about.

## 2. The world of Avarra

Avarra is a world of old kingdoms, independent cities, inhabited wilderness, and roads maintained by agreements older than their rulers. Different ancestries live throughout its communities. Neither ancestry nor class determines allegiance, morality, or whether Raphael chooses someone.

The first campaign takes place in the **Lantern Reach**, a river basin on the western edge of a broken empire. The River Vey connects mountain settlements, reed villages, Greyharbor, and the coast. Beyond the maintained roads, ancient boundaries between the mortal world and the realm of echoes have weakened.

Six great beacons protect the Reach. Each anchors the same ancient covenant, the **Concord of Witnesses**. Household lanterns borrow a small measure of their light. The beacons have become part of ordinary life: safe crossings, reliable landmarks, recognizable doorways, and the comfort of finding your way home.

People understand the covenant differently. A guild clerk calls it infrastructure. A priest calls it sacred hospitality. A Riverfolk elder calls it a promise that no king owns. All three are partly right.

### Ordinary life worth returning to

Greyharbor smells of wet stone, bread, lamp oil, and river mud. Its stairways carry flood marks rather than monuments to emperors. Bargemen trade songs as readily as news. Children build paper lanterns shaped like animals; merchants pretend not to notice when the children demand a festival discount.

News travels through ferries, travelers, posted notices, and the occasional costly magical message. Stories usually arrive before anyone agrees what happened. Money pays for goods, but a remembered favor can open doors that a purse cannot.

Each adventure should revisit one small recurring detail: the bakery that begins opening again, the ferryman who finally sings, the damaged school repaired with the party's reward. These changes make the campaign's history visible.

### Magic and memory

Ordinary fantasy magic exists alongside the covenant. The selected D&D rules govern spells, creatures, combat, and character abilities. Use the following setting facts consistently:

- The covenant's erasure affects recognition and belonging. It does not change what physically happened. Letters, buildings, objects, and contradictory accounts can survive.
- An erased person remains a person with agency. Others fail to recognize their identity or connection to a place. Severe breaches can strand them in the realm of echoes, called the **Pale Between**.
- Restoring recognition cannot reverse history or automatically restore someone who died. Ordinary resurrection magic follows the selected game rules.
- A willing personal account, supported by independent evidence, can help recover an identity. Forced declarations and invented stories cannot repair the covenant.
- **Hollows** are predatory echo-creatures formed at breaches. They are distinct from the people whose identities have been erased. Establish that distinction before presenting combat choices.
- Player abilities remain useful. Divination, restoration, translation, and other magic can reveal clues or solve local obstacles. Define their interaction with a particular scene before resolving a roll; never invent an immunity solely to protect a plot twist.

## 4. Raphael and the chosen few

Raphael is the **Witness Above the Lanterns**, an all-seeing divine presence. He knows the world's established past and present, including truths mortals have forgotten. He sees possible futures as branches; the future is not a completed story that the heroes must perform.

His choice falls on people who have already demonstrated that another person's fate matters to them. A hero might have returned for a stranger, preserved a disgraced person's testimony, kept a difficult promise, or admitted a costly truth. Let each player supply that moment. Being chosen confers responsibility and an unusual connection to Raphael, not political authority or guaranteed victory.

Each hero perceives him differently: words in a candle flame, a voice beneath running water, a familiar figure in a dream, or a warmth behind the heart. Other party members may hear the same counsel, or a hero may receive a private hint when the table supports private information. Raphael never turns private counsel into a command to betray another player.

His chosen retain their own observations of erased people. This gift explains why they notice what their neighbors overlook; it supplies no facts they never learned. Their testimony can help restore one person's recognition when independently supported, but an active, unrepaired amendment can erase that recognition again. Individual rescues therefore matter immediately while the wider problem remains.

### Why he guides indirectly

Raphael helped witness the original covenant. Its power rests on mortals freely recognizing one another. If he dictates the solution, obedience takes the place of that recognition. His restraint is a commitment to the heroes' authorship of the future.

He can draw attention to an earned clue, ask a difficult question, or suggest where a neglected connection might matter. He cannot make a choice on a hero's behalf, compel devotion, restore a failed mission by decree, or promise survival. Declining his advice does not revoke a character's divine worth or class abilities.

Raphael is patient, warm, and sparing with words. He remembers each hero's earlier concerns. He can admit sorrow and offer reassurance. Avoid riddles whose only purpose is to make a clear situation harder to understand.

### Counsel examples

| What the party has encountered | Counsel | What remains for the heroes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two witnesses describe different owners of the same empty house. | "You have heard who owns the house. You have not yet heard who waited at its door." | Find another perspective and decide whom to approach. |
| Lysa notices an extra verse in a familiar festival song. | "Some promises outlive the mouth that first spoke them. Ask who taught her the verse." | Discover the song's personal history. |
| The party debates an offer from Sable. | "You know what she asks of you. Make room to hear what she fears to lose." | Judge Sable after learning more; Raphael does not certify her honesty. |
| The heroes return after a setback. | "I saw you return for one another. Begin there. The road has not ended." | Choose a recovery, rescue, or repair mission. |

Repeated requests should refer back to earlier counsel and current discoveries. A hint may become clearer after new evidence, but asking again alone must not expose an unrevealed identity, betrayal, solution, or future result. Save who heard each hint and which facts supported it.

## 5. The Lantern Compact

### Player-requested scene images

At any time, any player may ask for an image of what their hero is looking at. This is a read-only visual aid with no action, resource, counsel-budget or in-world time cost. It remains available outside their turn and while play is paused. Depict their current position, senses, lighting and actually visible people, places and objects. A closer view illustrates already observable details; it does not grant a search, read an illegible clue, reveal a hidden creature or decide an outcome.

Use the campaign's Witnesslight style and approved location/character references. For split parties or private discoveries, use the requesting hero's observation record and deliver privately. Save the source event, view revision and exact prompt. If play changes during rendering, label the image as its request-time snapshot and let the player request the updated view. Existing art may be reused only when it fits that actual view. New player choices change the next projection rather than rewriting prior images.

The browser and Discord implementation is documented in [Show what I see](raphael-council/images/README.md). It accepts host-published observable views; this framework does not itself provide an automated narration/state feed.

Players may also ask how far a visible target is and whether their character can reach it. Answer from the current mapped positions and approved character mechanics. Separate distance, movement cost and weapon range; explain remaining movement, turn restrictions and known obstacles. These questions cost no action or game time and remain available during pauses. Use only observable information, label the source map revision, and ask for the host's ruling when a position or ability is not configured. Never derive exact measurements from the perspective of generated artwork or promise an action's success from range alone.

The party is a chartered, mixed-discipline fellowship based in **the Last Hearth**, a former riverside signal house. Its common room, infirmary, workshop, kitchen, and map table provide a recurring home between missions.

The Compact receives requests rather than absolute orders. Its charter permits it to cross local jurisdictions by invitation and assist people caught between rival authorities. It does not grant immunity from consequences. Local communities can refuse help, renegotiate terms, or criticize the heroes publicly.

Characters bring complementary specialties: a ranger who knows strange roads, a cleric who tends damaged bonds, a rogue who finds overlooked connections, a wizard who understands old wards, a fighter who steadies the group under pressure, or any other player-created role. No class is mandatory, and nobody is locked into one mission function.

Preparation scenes focus on purpose, known facts, relationships, magical resources, and acceptable costs. Field scenes involve investigation, negotiation, travel, exploration, fantasy hazards, and combat when events warrant it. Debriefs establish what actually happened and whose future has changed.

The Last Hearth develops with the campaign. Guests stay longer. A room becomes a school. A recovered artifact needs a custodian. A memorial gains a new name. The party decides what kind of institution it is building.

Household lanterns and local civic lamps are not additional great beacons.

## 7. Factions with reasons to exist

### Hearthbound League (`hearth`)

The guilds that feed, repair, and shelter Greyharbor. The League wants reliable services and fears being blamed for a catastrophe it cannot control. It offers supplies, craftsmen, introductions, and public legitimacy.

Its chair, **Mirelle Dain**, favors emergency central authority. Its neighborhood stewards fear permanent control. Neither side wants the city to burn. The League becomes dangerous when it treats dissent as a maintenance problem; it becomes a strong ally when the party helps it share responsibility.

### Riverfolk Council (`riverfolk`)

A federation of ferry families, floodplain communities, and elected delegates. It wants its settlements to keep a meaningful voice in the covenant. It offers passage, local knowledge, testimony, and homes beyond the city.

Speaker **Nera Venn** insists that assistance must not quietly become ownership. Younger delegates disagree about whether confrontation or compromise will secure that independence. A party that respects the Council's decisions can gain an enduring alliance without automatic agreement on every issue.

### Nightglass Court (`nightglass`)

A network of old houses, patrons, lenders, and collectors of inconvenient records. It wants influence over whoever controls the beacons. It offers rare documents, introductions, shelter, and resources others cannot provide openly.

The Court contains competing ambitions. Some members profit from emergency contracts. Others believe recovering the old covenant is their obligation. Its envoy **Sable** can be an ally, a rival, or both in different matters. The Court is not the single hidden author of every trouble.

### The Quiet Ministry

An emerging movement offering relief from fear, grief, and uncertainty. Its hospitals and shelters genuinely help people. Its senior officials secretly promote a covenant amendment that lets a central authority decide which memories are too disruptive to preserve.

The Ministry seeks voluntary supporters before reaching for coercive power. Its ordinary members know different amounts. Discovering its leadership's actions does not make every resident who accepted its help an enemy.

### The Unwritten

People whose identities have been removed from the covenant. They have no unified leader, doctrine, or alignment. Some seek restoration, some build new lives, and some distrust any institution that promises to name them again.

Their testimony is crucial, but they are characters with desires beyond explaining the mystery. A restored person may still refuse to go home.

## 9. DM truth: what is actually happening

The original Concord distributed authority among six communities. Every community retained the right to bear witness, disagree with the records, and withdraw its participation through a deliberate public process.

The Witness Vault preserves those accounts. After the Great Flood, Greyharbor gradually treated the Vault as an administrative system rather than a living agreement. Provisioning contracts became entangled with recognition rights.

Rook's **Continuance Amendment** redirects contested identities into a protective holding process under a central custodian. Edran Voss occupies that role. The first affected people are those whose identities have been associated with disputed debts or conflicting records. The festival deadline will widen the affected group if the amendment is renewed.

Voss sincerely believes he is preserving people from a world too dangerous to trust. His solution is a frozen, managed society in which no disagreement can threaten stability. What he calls shelter gradually becomes imprisonment in the Pale Between.

Three truths remain fixed throughout the campaign:

1. The households' missing people were not invented, and the past has not been rewritten.
2. The amendment exploits a damaged institution. Exposing one criminal does not automatically repair every beacon or restore every identity.
3. The original covenant can be renegotiated. The heroes can preserve it, reform it, replace parts of it, or help communities leave it.

Raphael knows these truths but reveals them through earned, partial counsel. The heroes discover them through people, places, magic, and consequences. Do not change a culprit or explanation because the party correctly guessed it early.

### Evidence routes

| Revelation | Independent ways to learn it |
| --- | --- |
| Missing identities remain physically real. | Tavin and his ferry records; an unclaimed room at the Last Hearth; the same absent person's handwriting in two unrelated households. |
| The amendment links debts to recognition. | Oren's copy; Nera's grandmother's account compared with the new contract; a beacon maintenance inscription interpreted with appropriate skill or magic. |
| Rook knowingly inserted the clause. | His countersigned instruction preserved by a clerk; his own admission under credible questioning; testimony from the ritual scribe who received the altered text. |
| Voss controls the holding process. | A freed witness describes his voice; Glassmere's custodian record identifies the imprint; a supervised encounter with the Witness Vault reveals who is receiving appeals. |
| Community consent can change the covenant. | An original witness bell's dedication; a surviving Thornwake agreement; an elder's account corroborated by an independent Archive fragment. |

No essential revelation depends on one successful die roll, one living NPC, or one chosen mission. Failure may change time, trust, exposure, or the form of evidence. Preserve other established routes and let player abilities create new ones.

## 10. The opening missions

Present these as simultaneous requests on Day 6. They connect to the same crisis through different communities. The party may choose any order, divide responsibilities if the table supports it, or invent another response. Unchosen missions progress through their stated deadlines; they do not disappear when the council favors something else.

### The Lantern Accord (`accord`)

**Request:** Nera asks the party to help Bellweather resolve a debt before its creditor withholds the festival lamp allotment at sunset on Day 8.

**Immediate objective:** Secure a workable settlement that the residents accept, and establish why the contract lists an unrecognized resident as guarantor.

**Scenes available:** A heated assembly in a ferry house; a creditor with a contract she inherited rather than wrote; a visit to Tavin, whose name still appears on an old boat permit; a festival rehearsal where Lysa hears her missing verse.

**The complication:** The debt covered real repairs. Canceling it without addressing those costs leaves someone else responsible. Its recognition clause is separate from the legitimate bill and can be challenged independently.

**Branches:** Negotiate a revised agreement; arrange shared repayment and independent maintenance; demonstrate the clause's invalidity through records; ask the community to propose its own settlement. A purchased solution may be fast but consume party resources. A public challenge may establish a useful precedent but require more testimony.

**Persistent consequences:** Record the legal settlement, who bore its cost, what Bellweather requested, whether Tavin was recognized, and which relationships changed. Do not equate favoring the League with automatic moral success.

**If unresolved:** At the deadline the lamps are withheld and the festival moves to ordinary candlelight. The next world turn increases the local recognition threat. The settlement remains inhabited and can still be helped; the lost festival becomes something people remember.

### The Quiet Archive (`archive`)

**Request:** Oren asks the party to recover a missing civic record before a hearing on Day 9 condemns his mentor for falsifying provisioning accounts.

**Immediate objective:** Establish what the record actually says and who had access to it.

**Scenes available:** The mentor's public hearing; a lower reading room whose water reflects absent readers; an interview with a clerk embarrassed by her own mistake; a bronze witness bell that repeats testimony only when addressed by its original civic title.

**The complication:** Oren's mentor did conceal a record to protect a debtor's identity. That misconduct is distinct from the amendment's insertion. Clearing the false accusation does not require pretending the mentor did nothing wrong.

**Branches:** Recover the original; corroborate Oren's copy with independent records; obtain the scribe's account; seek an adjournment while protecting witnesses. Appropriate spells may secure evidence quickly, leaving its interpretation and public consequences as the challenge.

**Persistent consequences:** Save the evidence's contents, who possesses copies, the hearing's finding, the mentor's status, and who now knows about the amendment. Sable's response depends on which Court interests were actually affected.

**If unresolved:** The mentor loses office and the Ministry offers to administer the collection. Evidence survives elsewhere. The next mission may become an appeal, a witness protection request, or a negotiation for access.

### The Ember Vigil (`vigil`)

**Request:** Ember Crossing asks for relief before the watch's next public emergency assembly on the morning of Day 8.

**Immediate objective:** Explain the alarms and restore enough confidence for the town to make an informed decision about its safety.

**Scenes available:** A watchkeeper afraid to admit exhaustion; a returning traveler recognized by only one neighbor; a beacon chamber ringing with old distress calls; a Hollow forming where the ward repeats a failed warning.

**The complication:** The watch has mistaken some echoes for present danger, but a genuine breach is developing. Declaring every alarm false is as misleading as treating every sound as an attack.

**Branches:** Investigate the repeating signals; protect people while a specialist examines the ward; confront the Hollow; help residents relocate temporarily while the beacon is repaired. Combat can remove an immediate threat without settling the underlying civic dispute.

**Persistent consequences:** Record the breach's condition, displaced households, the watch's credibility, local decisions, and any identity testimony recovered. Successfully protecting residents can build confidence even if the damaged beacon takes longer to repair.

**If unresolved:** The assembly authorizes temporary relocation and receives a Ministry offer. Some residents accept, others stay. Their later choices depend on the party's actual contact with them, not a universal hostility penalty.

## 11. The campaign's larger shape

Treat these movements as changes in scope. Their order and duration follow discoveries and decisions rather than fixed session numbers.

### Movement I: Someone is missing

The heroes establish the Last Hearth and become attached to Greyharbor. The opening missions reveal that apparently unrelated disputes share an altered recognition process. A natural climax is a public choice at the Festival of Returning Lights: expose an incomplete finding, protect people first, or buy time for firmer evidence.

Advance when the party has evidence of systemic harm and decides how to respond. A skipped festival or early revelation changes the transition; it does not break the campaign.

### Movement II: Who has the right to remember?

Travel to Thornwake, Bellweather, and other beacon communities reveals incompatible experiences of the covenant. The Compact must earn permission, reunite witnesses, and choose which institutions to support. Each hero's unfinished promise should connect to one local relationship without making every backstory secretly caused by Voss.

The movement turns when the party understands that the covenant can be changed and that communities disagree about the desirable replacement. An alliance may form; a coalition may split; one community may withdraw. All are viable developments.

### Movement III: The mercy of the Hollow Regent

Glassmere offers a disturbing promise: nobody inside its mirror halls feels abandoned, because their lives are held at the moment before loss. Some captives want to leave. Some are frightened of leaving. Some supporters arrived willingly and did not understand the terms.

The heroes can rescue witnesses, negotiate access, expose Rook, confront the Ministry's internal divisions, or challenge Voss directly. Knowing his identity does not obligate an immediate battle. His imprint may be contained, stripped of authority, persuaded to relinquish a power, or destroyed through the selected game's fantasy mechanics.

### Movement IV: A covenant the living can carry

The climax concerns both the immediate threat and who will govern what remains. The heroes need not secure unanimous agreement before saving people. Partial settlements can create a patchwork Reach with new advantages and new problems.

Possible settlements include a repaired distributed covenant, locally independent beacon agreements, a public institution with limited delegated powers, or communities accepting less magical protection in exchange for leaving the network. No single settlement is the author's predetermined correct answer.

Resolve the fates of named people and institutions before narrating a broad epilogue. An ending that returns Tavin but divides the Council differs from one that reunites the Reach while Sable's niece remains missing. Continue from those facts if the table wants another season.

Raphael's final counsel should acknowledge a particular choice the heroes actually made:

> "I remember the name you refused to leave behind. Whatever follows, this world will carry that kindness."

## 12. A world that advances between sessions

Advance the world when time passes in the fiction, not while the players are offline. A week between sessions may represent no in-world time. A deliberate three-day recovery means three days for other people as well.

For each relevant interval, choose developments supported by a faction's goal, opportunity, resources, and knowledge. A rival can act independently, but cannot respond to a secret the rival has not learned. Telegraph major developments through rumors, invitations, shortages, visible repairs, or testimony before a deadline closes.

Use four opening threat clocks, measured from 0 to 6. Advance at most one step per three in-world days when the stated cause remains active; a published deadline may trigger a specific scene sooner. Missions can pause, reverse, complete, or replace a clock when their results justify it.

| Clock and starting value | Cause of advancement | Escalation and resolution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lost Names: 1/6** | The amended recognition process continues in affected communities. | At 3, independent households report contradictions; at 5, a group becomes stranded in the Pale Between; at 6, a beacon district suffers a major loss of recognition. This creates a recovery crisis, not an automatic campaign ending. Correcting the process halts new losses; existing victims still need resolution. |
| **Ministry Mandate: 2/6** | The Ministry provides unchallenged emergency services and obtains new agreements. | At 3, it receives Archive access; at 5, a civic vote considers expanded powers; at 6, the specific approving institution transfers those powers. Public evidence, viable alternatives, and internal dissent can change the vote. Its authority does not magically extend to communities that refused. |
| **Lantern Fracture: 2/6** | Unrepaired beacon damage continues to strain the network. | At 3, travel disruptions become visible; at 5, a neighboring ward becomes unreliable; at 6, one established link fails. Named routes and places change. Repairs and negotiated alternatives can stabilize individual links. |
| **Reach Divided: 1/6** | A documented dispute remains unanswered or a faction exploits a known grievance. | At 3, a delegate withdraws cooperation; at 5, separate agreements emerge; at 6, the current regional coalition dissolves. Local relationships survive and a new coalition remains possible. Do not advance this simply because the party disagreed with an official. |

Recovery, hospitality, and successful aid should also create positive developments: a restored ferry, an apprentice who becomes a capable caretaker, an ally who solves a problem without summoning the party. Every world turn need not introduce another disaster.

### What the campaign retains

Retain actual facts alongside abstract values: mission outcomes; discovered and unrevealed clues; names and locations; rumors and who believes them; promises and deadlines; wounds and recovery; expended or recovered resources; relationships; faction agreements; repaired routes; Raphael's counsel; and the evidence behind each lasting change.

For each important mission, describe an immediate consequence, a later possible consequence with a trigger, and a public account. A later possibility remains a possibility until its trigger occurs. Public belief may disagree with the factual record.

Example: The party recovers a record and gives Oren a copy. That happens now. The possibility that Mirelle changes her vote becomes real only when she sees credible evidence. The rumor that the Compact stole civic secrets may spread if a witness reports the retrieval that way. Keep all three distinct.

### Compatibility with the current seed

The application begins on Day 6 with readiness 72 and attention 38. Its starting faction values are Hearthbound trust 62, Nightglass influence 37, and Riverfolk trust 68. Place values are Greyharbor stability 64, Archive confidence 47, and Crossing readiness 71. Lysa, Oren, and Sable begin at trust 58, 51, and 34.

Those measurements have different meanings. Nightglass influence is not its affection for the party. Public attention is visibility and can attract help as well as scrutiny. A low relationship value is uncertainty or distrust, not permission to make that character automatically hostile.

The current application uses fixed demonstration outcomes. This framework supplies the story distinctions a later implementation must represent; its secret history, clocks, missions, and branching outcomes are not yet automatically loaded by the app.

## 13. How the council develops the story together

Aster, Mnemos, Seren, Kael, and Mira are the application's collaborating story roles. They are not five additional gods secretly directing the heroes. Raphael is the voice the chosen encounter within the fiction.

| Council member | Story responsibility | Required contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aster, World Steward** | Communities, geography, institutions, independent activity. | Identify who is affected and which world facts permit the proposed development. |
| **Mnemos, Memory Keeper** | History, clues, knowledge, promises, established outcomes. | Confirm continuity and identify any protected revelation. |
| **Seren, Consequence Keeper** | Costs, risk, recovery, lasting effects. | Offer plausible immediate effects and conditional later effects, including a constructive outcome. |
| **Kael, Mission Architect** | Purpose, scene structure, obstacles, approach diversity. | Give each proposed scene a playable objective and more than one feasible approach. |
| **Mira, Party Advocate** | Character motives, meaningful choices, spotlight, player intent. | Identify whose personal stake is engaged and whether the party can reasonably refuse or redirect the scene. |

For each consequential development, build a bounded tree: current situation, several candidate scenes, possible responses, and short consequence summaries. Council members assess the candidates independently using their responsibilities. Compare supporting facts, challenge contradictions, revise viable candidates, and use equal votes to recommend a direction.

Variability should change substance. For example, an invitation to a public hearing, an encounter with a displaced family, and a request to repair a beacon each expose a different part of the crisis. Merely shuffling their display order does not create another story.

Preserve the same historical truth beneath these branches. If the party befriends Sable, the council may develop a family rescue lead. If it rejects her offer, the evidence about Glassmere can still emerge through a freed witness. Neither branch changes who her niece is or what already happened to her.

Only the encountered branch enters the factual history. Unchosen possible outcomes stay hypothetical. Faction actions can become facts through the world-turn process without player approval, provided they follow established time, knowledge, and capabilities. The party controls its own actions and can propose options beyond the menu.

Keep a concise record of proposals, evidence, assessments, disagreement, and the selected development. Raphael expresses the resulting scene and offers permitted counsel. His narration cannot silently overrule an established fact or add an unagreed consequence.

### Information boundaries

The DM's author record contains all fixed secrets. Each NPC has a separate knowledge record. Each party member has an observation record; the party's shared record contains what they have actually shared.

Council analysis may use DM facts when needed, but the player view must contain only disclosed options and visible stakes. Do not put the identity of a hidden antagonist into a player-visible score explanation, council transcript, export, or hint. Raphael's apparent omniscience belongs to the fiction; what he says remains limited by the counsel policy.

## 14. A renewable mission framework

Build a mission from a person with a need, a place under pressure, an objective the party can understand, a deadline with an established cause, an obstacle, several plausible approaches, and consequences that matter after the scene.

Alternate between kinds of play so the campaign does not become a succession of hearings or battles:

- **Recovery:** Find a person who remembers a home that has forgotten them.
- **Protection:** Accompany a witness who can speak for themselves and may disagree with the party's plan.
- **Discovery:** Explore a damaged beacon to learn which promise it preserves.
- **Negotiation:** Help two communities solve a real conflict while a rival offers an easier bargain.
- **Confrontation:** Face a Hollow, a corrupted guardian, or an accountable human adversary whose defeat changes a specific threat.
- **Restoration:** Reopen a route, recover a name, repair a bond, or help residents return after displacement.
- **Personal obligation:** Follow a hero's chosen promise while another member of the company takes the lead.

At least every few missions, return to the Last Hearth for ordinary life. Let the heroes enjoy a success before placing the next request on the table. Refusal can have consequences without becoming an accusation that the party is playing incorrectly.

## 15. Opening scene: the unlit lantern

Read or adapt this after the players introduce their heroes and their connection to the Last Hearth:

> Rain ticks against the map-room windows. Below them, the River Vey carries a hundred little reflections toward the sea.
>
> Lysa sets a paper lantern on the table. Its painted bird is crooked. Whoever made it pressed too hard with the brush.
>
> "It won't take a flame," she says. "That happens. Damp paper. Bad wick. But I can't remember who gave it to me."
>
> You can.
>
> Yesterday, a ferryman brought it to the door. He had river mud on his boots and a joke that made Lysa laugh before she asked his name.
>
> Beside the lantern lie three requests: Bellweather's debt, Oren's missing record, Ember Crossing's alarms.
>
> For a moment, the rain grows quiet.
>
> Raphael speaks, in the way each of you has come to recognize.
>
> "Begin with the person, chosen ones. The light remembers why it was lit."
>
> Lysa looks from one of you to the next. "Did you hear something?"

Ask what the heroes remember about meeting the ferryman, then what they do. Player-created details can enrich his manner or the meeting; preserve the established identity and history. The party can follow him, examine the lantern, question Lysa, open the requests, or propose something else. The campaign begins with that choice.
