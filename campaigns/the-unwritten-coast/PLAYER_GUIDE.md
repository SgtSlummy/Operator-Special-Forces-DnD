# OPERATOR: THE UNWRITTEN COAST

## Your first briefing

The rain has been falling for seventeen days. In Brinewatch, people argue about whether that is a record while moving their chairs away from the leaks.

At dusk, a ferry arrives without a harbor flag. Its passengers say they have come from Bellwether, a town six miles across the bay. The official harbor map shows open water where the town should be.

The passengers know their own homes. Someone has to believe them.

You are at the quay when the ferry's mooring breaks.

## What kind of story are we making?

A strange coastal adventure about a small company doing difficult work for ordinary people. Expect rescues, investigations, journeys, tense negotiations, dangerous places, complicated loyalties and time to get attached to your companions. There is room for jokes, meals and small victories alongside the storm.

You decide what your character believes and does. Plans can fail; good ideas can work without a fight. Your choices about people, property, promises and evidence will change later adventures. There is no required personality, faction allegiance or predetermined sacrifice.

The series is designed for twelve adventures, usually 2–3 hours each, beginning at level 3. We will agree the exact D&D edition and character options before play. Suggested party size is two to four. Everyone advances together for meaningful accomplishments, including successful negotiation and rescue.

## Make a person, then make an operator

Choose or invent one reason you are near the ferry:

- **The returning local:** you left this coast and have come back for someone. You decide who and why.
- **The practical professional:** you know boats, repairs, medicine, logistics or fieldwork. Your expertise is useful to people who cannot afford it.
- **The lapsed official:** you used to work for a harbor institution. You decide what you left and whether the departure was friendly.
- **The traveler with a debt:** someone here once helped you. You have arrived with the means or intention to repay them.
- **The stranger who stayed:** you planned to pass through Brinewatch. A person or a promise has kept you here.

These are story hooks, not character classes or compulsory backstories. Any agreed class can contribute. Choose your own species, name, skills and history using the table's character rules.

Answer four short questions:

1. What useful thing do you do when everyone else panics?
2. What ordinary comfort will you make room for on a bad day?
3. Which other character do you already know—or want to get to know—and what have you actually agreed happened between you?
4. What kind of promise is hard for you to make?

A private character secret is optional. Give the GM permission for any part you want brought into play. A hook never authorizes the GM to invent a hidden betrayal, kill a relative, rewrite a memory or choose your reaction.

## The company belongs to you

After the opening rescue, the company can acquire a name, a shared table and a place to keep its gear. “Operator” describes people who can get things done; it does not require a military chain of command.

Agree three charter lines together:

> We accept work when __________.
>
> We will not abandon __________.
>
> When our employer and the people we help disagree, we __________.

You may amend the charter through play. No single player gains control of another player's character by holding a rank. You can accept, refuse or renegotiate a job. Tell the GM what you intend so the world can respond.

## What everyone here knows

**Brinewatch** is a busy harbor city kept running by fishers, dockworkers, artisans and an overworked civic administration. Its Crown Beacon shines over the harbor.

**Bellwether** is remembered by ordinary people as a ferry town across the bay. Its disappearance from the official map is the immediate mystery, not proof that the passengers are lying.

**The Crooked Oar** is a waterside boardinghouse with a kitchen that feeds people before discussing the bill. Its keeper, Mara Dain, has strong opinions about wet boots.

**The coast's beacons** are part of its storm defenses. Most citizens know their lights and bells; few know their engineering.

The map supplied at the table shows what your characters can currently know. It will grow as you explore. Personal notes and character sheets remain reliable records of what you have actually learned; a strange event in the story does not secretly rewrite them.

## Our table agreement

Before play, settle the rules edition, allowed character material, how dice are rolled, the desired level of peril and the handling of disagreements. Discuss comfort around drowning, displacement, injury and coercive officials. Change the framing or fade out details when needed. Anyone can request a pause without their character suffering for it.

Speaking in character is welcome, but describing an intention plainly works just as well. Nobody needs an accent, a performance background or an exhaustive biography. Make room for each other; the GM will ask for intentions from quieter players too.

Recording or AI-assisted summaries require the actual table's consent process. They are optional. At the end of a session, check the recap: what happened, what you suspect and what remains undecided.

## At the water's edge

> The ferry strikes the quay hard enough to scatter mugs from a nearby stall. A woman on deck holds a lantern in both hands. Its light points sideways, toward the empty bay.
>
> “Please,” she calls. “The rest of them are still there.”
>
> Then the rope snaps.

What do you do?
